# Prototype Drip Irrigation Operating and Troubleshooting SOP

> **Prototype knowledge document for demonstration purposes.** It does not replace the system designer's manual, safety procedures, water-quality plan, or local regulations. Isolate energy and pressure before servicing equipment.

## Objective

Operate a drip or microirrigation zone predictably, verify that intended water is delivered, identify abnormalities early, and leave a traceable record. Follow manufacturer limits for pump, filter, regulator, pipe, valves, and emitters.

## Before starting

1. **Confirm the plan:** field/zone, crop, required depth or volume, planned duration, and current root-zone need.
2. **Check water and energy availability:** confirm source level, pump readiness, electrical or fuel condition, and safe access.
3. **Inspect intake and filtration:** check screen, media, disc, or other filters as applicable. Use differential-pressure or manufacturer criteria rather than guessing filter condition.
4. **Inspect the network:** look for damaged mains, submains, laterals, loose fittings, open flush ends, kinked tubing, and disturbed emitters.
5. **Set valves correctly:** confirm the intended zone is open and unintended paths are closed. Never exceed rated pressure.
6. **Check instruments:** pressure gauges and flow measurement should be readable and plausible.
7. **Review water quality and treatment:** use only approved, system-specific treatment procedures. Do not improvise chemical injection.

## Start-up and observation

Start the pump or open supply according to its approved procedure. Increase pressure in a controlled manner where the equipment requires it. Observe inlet pressure, downstream pressure, flow, filter differential, leaks, unusual noise, vibration, and zone behaviour. Walk representative upstream, middle, and downstream positions. Confirm emitters discharge and the wetted pattern is developing where roots can use it.

## If pressure is low

Do not merely increase pressure. Check, in a safe order:

- source level and pump status, power, priming, rotation, or other manufacturer indicators;
- incorrectly positioned valves, an accidentally open flush point, or too many zones operating;
- a broken pipe, disconnected lateral, large leak, or damaged fitting;
- abnormal filter condition and pressure difference;
- blocked intake, supply restriction, regulator problem, or gauge error;
- whether measured flow is unusually high (often consistent with a leak/open path) or unusually low (often consistent with restriction, pump/supply, or closed-valve problems).

USDA-NRCS notes that pressure changes can indicate leaks, component or line breaks, blocked filters, or pump malfunction. Exact diagnosis depends on where pressure and flow are measured.

## If pressure is high

Stop or isolate the affected operation if limits may be exceeded. Check regulator operation, downstream valve state, blocked paths, gauge accuracy, and whether the correct zone configuration is open. High pressure can damage components and does not safely solve emitter blockage.

## If application is non-uniform

Compare pressure and discharge at representative head, middle, and tail locations. Inspect clogged or damaged emitters, lateral length/configuration, elevation effects, filtration, pressure regulation, leaks, and root intrusion where relevant. Use manufacturer-approved flushing and maintenance. Do not enlarge emitter openings manually.

## Shutdown

Complete flushing only under the approved design procedure. Close or stop the system in the specified sequence to avoid damaging pressure transients. Restore valves and flush caps, inspect leaks, and leave equipment safe. Record actual start/stop time, flow or volume, pressure readings, filter observation, maintenance, abnormal events, and the operator.

## Escalate and do not run when

There is exposed or unsafe electrical equipment, a major uncontrolled leak, pressure outside component ratings, a suspected chemical-injection fault, contaminated or unsuitable water, pump cavitation or severe vibration, structural damage, or an unresolved discrepancy between instruments and field observations.
