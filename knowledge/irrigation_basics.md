# Irrigation Scheduling Basics

> **Prototype knowledge document for demonstration purposes.** It is educational, not a field prescription. Validate decisions with local agronomic guidance, soil measurements, crop variety, water quality, regulations, and equipment specifications.

## Purpose

Irrigation scheduling answers two linked questions: **when should water be applied, and how much should be applied?** A useful schedule protects the crop from damaging water stress while avoiding runoff, prolonged saturation, unnecessary pumping, nutrient movement below the root zone, and disease-favouring conditions.

## The root-zone water balance

Think of the active root zone as a water account. Rainfall and irrigation are deposits. Crop evapotranspiration (ETc), runoff, and deep drainage are withdrawals. FAO defines irrigation need broadly as crop water need minus effective rainfall. A daily field balance can track yesterday's deficit, add crop water use, and subtract effective rain and irrigation.

ETc changes with crop type, growth stage, canopy development, and weather. Reference evapotranspiration reflects atmospheric demand; a crop coefficient adapts it to a particular crop and stage. Temperature, solar radiation, humidity, and wind all influence demand. Effective rainfall is only the portion stored where roots can use it—not every millimetre recorded by a rain gauge.

## Soil controls how much water can be stored

- **Field capacity** is the approximate water content after excess gravitational water has drained.
- **Permanent wilting point** is the lower limit below which plants cannot readily recover water.
- **Total available water** is the water between those limits within the effective rooting depth.
- **Allowable depletion** is the fraction that may be used before irrigation should begin. It is crop-, stage-, soil-, climate-, and management-dependent.

Sandy soils commonly store less plant-available water and may need smaller, more frequent applications. Finer soils can store more, but poor structure or slow infiltration can cause runoff or ponding if application rate is too high. Compaction, salinity, slope, and spatial variation matter. Rooting depth changes during crop development, so the managed reservoir is not fixed throughout the season.

## Monitoring methods

Use more than one signal where practical:

1. **Soil-moisture sensing:** place calibrated sensors at representative locations and useful depths in the active root zone. Check that readings match field observations.
2. **Weather or ET water balance:** update rainfall, irrigation, and estimated ETc regularly.
3. **Field inspection:** inspect plant condition, soil wetting depth, runoff, ponding, leaks, and emitter uniformity.
4. **Applied-water measurement:** use flow meters, pressure readings, run time, and known system discharge rather than assuming delivery.

One volumetric moisture percentage is not a universal trigger. Sensor type, installation, soil texture, bulk density, salinity, placement, and calibration change the meaning of the reading. Establish field-specific trigger and refill points with local professional guidance.

## A practical scheduling workflow

1. Identify the crop, current growth stage, effective root depth, soil texture, and irrigation method.
2. Estimate current root-zone water status from calibrated sensors and/or a water balance.
3. Include recent effective rainfall and credible near-term rainfall information; do not count forecast rain as delivered water.
4. Estimate crop water use using locally appropriate ET data or extension guidance.
5. Select a trigger that protects the crop before damaging stress, especially in sensitive stages.
6. Limit the net application to the root-zone deficit. Account separately for system efficiency and unavoidable operational losses when determining gross application.
7. Confirm pump, filter, pressure, valves, supply, and distribution condition before operation.
8. Observe the irrigation, record actual duration and volume, then verify the resulting wetting depth.

## Crop stage and risk

Sensitivity to shortage changes through a season. FAO training guidance identifies flowering as particularly sensitive for several crops, including tomato, and flowering/boll formation for cotton. This does not mean other stages can be ignored; it means a scheduler should use stage-aware risk and avoid blindly applying one threshold all season.

## Conditions requiring caution or escalation

Pause and investigate when sensors disagree strongly, readings are implausibly static, water quality or salinity is uncertain, rain is causing saturation, runoff begins, pressure is abnormal, water fails to reach the intended root zone, or disease risk makes wetting practice consequential.
