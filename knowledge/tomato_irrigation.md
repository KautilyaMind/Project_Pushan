# Tomato Irrigation: Stage-Aware Prototype Guidance

> **Prototype knowledge document for demonstration purposes.** It is not a universal tomato schedule or agronomic standard. Adapt it to cultivar, production system, soil, rooting depth, climate, water quality, disease pressure, and local recommendations.

## Why consistency matters

Tomato water demand changes as the canopy and fruit load develop. FAO material identifies flowering as especially sensitive to water shortage and reports that processing-tomato seasonal consumption can vary widely with climate, crop type, soil, irrigation, and management. Therefore, a fixed calendar or a single uncalibrated soil-moisture percentage is not a dependable prescription.

## Establishment

After transplanting, keep the young root zone adequately moist without maintaining waterlogged soil. The first objective is root establishment and good soil-to-root contact. Apply water within the developing root zone and check actual wetting depth. Small plants have a limited active root volume; excessive applications may drain below it.

## Vegetative development

As roots and leaf area expand, monitor a deeper portion of the root zone and update estimated ETc. Encourage an adequately wetted root zone rather than repeatedly wetting only the immediate surface. Adjust frequency for soil storage: coarse soils normally require closer observation and smaller applications than soils with greater available-water capacity.

## Flowering and fruit set

Treat flowering as a high-attention period. FAO training guidance ranks tomato flowering as more sensitive to shortage than yield formation. Avoid allowing a preventable root-zone deficit to become crop stress. This is **not** a recommendation to saturate the soil: poor aeration, runoff, and uncontrolled wetness are also undesirable.

For Pushan, a question such as “What soil moisture level is recommended during flowering?” should not produce one universal percentage. A responsible response is to use a locally calibrated trigger tied to field capacity, allowable depletion, root depth, soil type, and sensor placement, with tighter monitoring during flowering. Confirm the trigger with local agronomic advice.

## Fruit enlargement and harvest period

Continue balancing ETc, effective rain, and measured root-zone status. Canopy demand and weather can be substantial, but the required interval and depth remain site-specific. Abrupt changes in water availability can disrupt crop performance, so record irrigation and inspect soil response rather than responding only to surface appearance.

## Monitoring checklist

- Confirm crop stage and representative sensor placement.
- Inspect sensor status and compare multiple depths or locations when available.
- Track rain, irrigation, and ET-based depletion.
- Check root-zone wetting after an irrigation; avoid deep drainage beyond the intended zone.
- Inspect for wilting in context: midday leaf response alone can be misleading under high atmospheric demand.
- Check emitter flow and pressure so a scheduled application is actually delivered uniformly.
- Record duration and measured volume for later evaluation.

## Drip considerations

Drip can apply frequent, targeted water, but it depends on filtration, suitable pressure, functioning emitters, and placement that matches the root system. Inspect for leaks, blocked emitters, and unusually dry or saturated plants. Irrigation decisions should use the wetting pattern around the emitter, not an unrelated dry surface location.

## What Pushan should and should not infer

Pushan may explain that flowering deserves closer moisture monitoring and retrieve the factors used to establish a trigger. It should not claim that a prototype rule, a raw sensor percentage, or an LLM answer is an official crop standard. Local calibration and professional judgment remain required.
